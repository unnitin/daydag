# The pyramid: testing the argument

Pass 1 works from the titles alone. Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle (McKinsey, 1960s
onward; *The Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing and Thinking*) is the test. In her
words it is "a process for organizing your thinking": group low-level facts you see as
similar, draw an insight from the similarity, and repeat until the thinking "ends in a
single pyramid of ideas"; then "communicating the thinking requires only that you guide
the reader down the pyramid". A deck is reviewed by checking that its titles form one
pyramid, read from the top.

## Checklist

1. From the titles alone, write the governing thought in one sentence. If you cannot,
   stop: that is the first blocker.
2. Write the audience's question. Does the governing thought answer it, with a magnitude
   they care about (budget, revenue, a date), not only an activity?
3. Is it on the first content page?
4. Key lines: 3 to 5, of one kind (one plural noun covers them), no overlap, no gap, in
   an order you can name (time, structure, degree, or deductive).
5. Under each key line, do the slide titles add up to it, and only to it?
6. Any intellectually blank title ("three issues", "seven actions")? Any topic label?
   Any group of actions summarised as an activity instead of its effect?
7. Does each title connect to the one before it, in its terms?
8. Does the opening (SCQA) restate what the audience accepts and raise the question the
   answer answers, without bringing disputable news?
9. Is the last body page the decision or next steps, with owners and dates?
10. Is anything in the main flow supporting no key line (appendix or cut), or anything
    behind the appendix divider that the argument needs (move it up)?

## The structure, mapped to a deck

```
Governing thought          the deck's answer: executive summary, often the deck title
├─ Key line 1              section claim, or one numbered line of the summary
│  ├─ supporting claim     a slide title
│  │   └─ evidence         that slide's exhibit
│  └─ supporting claim
├─ Key line 2
└─ Key line 3
Decision / next steps      what the audience does with the answer
```

- **Governing thought**: the single sentence the audience must leave with. It answers the
  question the audience has, not the question the author found interesting.
- **Key lines**: the 3 to 5 ideas directly under it, which together are the reason to
  believe it (or the steps to carry it out). On a deck they are the summary's numbered
  lines and the section openers.
- **Slide titles** sit under a key line; **exhibits** sit under a title. The exhibit is the
  bottom of the pyramid: it is evidence, never a new claim.

## Minto's three rules, as review checks

1. **"Ideas at any level in the pyramid must always be summaries of the ideas grouped
   below them."** Read a key line, then the titles under it: do those titles, and only
   those, add up to it? A key line that says more than its slides prove is unsupported;
   slides that prove something the key line does not say are a detour or a missing key
   line.
2. **Ideas in each grouping are the same kind of idea.** Her test: you "should be able to
   classify it by the same plural noun as the first". Siblings are all reasons, or all
   steps, or all problems, or all options. A group that mixes a cause, a recommendation and
   a status update is three groups.
3. **Ideas in each grouping are logically ordered.** In an order you can name (below). If
   you cannot name it, the grouping is a list, not an argument.

The question to put to any group, in Minto's form: "Why have I brought together these
particular ideas and no others?" There are two good answers: they share one
characteristic (and the summary names it), or they are actions that together produce one
effect (and the summary names the effect). Any other answer means the group is not one.

## Vertical logic: the question-and-answer dialogue

Each statement raises a question in the reader's mind (why? how? how do you know? so
what?), and the level below answers exactly that question. Checks:

- Read the governing thought and write the question it raises. The key lines should
  answer that question, not another one. A deck whose summary says "we should retire the
  old warehouse" raises "why?" (or "how?"); key lines that instead describe the history of
  the warehouse answer neither.
- One question per level. Siblings answering different questions (two "why" and one
  "how") is a structure problem, usually fixed by moving the "how" down a level or into
  its own section.
- The same test on every slide: the title raises a question, the exhibit answers it.

## Horizontal logic: deductive or inductive

A grouping is "a clear inductive or deductive argument, one or the other, but not both at
once". In a deductive group each point derives from the one before; in an inductive group
they do not. Moving up, a deductive group is summarised by its conclusion; an inductive
group by the inference drawn from what its points have in common.

- **Inductive** grouping: parallel ideas of the same kind that together support the point
  above (three reasons, four levers). Easier to read; the right default for key lines.
  Check: one plural noun covers them; none overlaps another; nothing important is missing.
- **Deductive** grouping: a chain (a statement, a second statement about it, therefore
  a conclusion). Each step depends on the one before, so the reader must hold the chain;
  it fails if any link fails. Check: the conclusion follows from the premises, the
  premises are proven, and the chain is no longer than three or four steps. A long
  deductive chain at the key-line level makes the audience wait for the answer; put the
  conclusion first and the chain under it.

## MECE

Minto's own term (she has said she invented it). The key lines, and the slides in each group, are mutually exclusive (no overlap) and
collectively exhaustive (no gap, for the question being answered). Tests:

- **Overlap**: could one fact sit under two siblings? Then the split is wrong, or two
  siblings are one.
- **Gap**: what would a sceptic say is missing? The usual gaps are the option not
  considered, the cost not counted, the risk not named and the stakeholder not asked
  (see `attack.md`).
- **Level**: siblings are the same size of idea. "Revenue, costs, and the Q3 invoice
  error" mixes a category with an instance.

## The orders

A deductive group carries its own order (premise, premise, therefore). An inductive group
takes one of three, which come from the three ways of analysing: cause and effect gives
time order, dividing a whole into parts gives structural order, and classifying like
things gives degree order.

| Order | Use when the group is | Reviewer asks |
|---|---|---|
| Time (chronological) | steps, phases, a sequence of causes and effects; "a grouping of steps that represents a process or system always goes in time order" | Is this the real order of events or actions? |
| Structure | parts of a whole: business units, regions, systems, stages of a flow | Does the order follow the structure the audience already knows? |
| Degree (comparative, order of importance) | items of one kind compared on one measure: biggest first, most urgent first | Is the ranking stated, and is the biggest first? |
| Deductive | premises leading to a conclusion | Does it follow, and is the conclusion first in the deck? |

Unordered groups ("some thoughts", "other considerations") are a finding.

## SCQA: the opening

The executive summary or opening slides follow Situation, Complication, Question, Answer:

- **Situation**: something the audience already knows and accepts. Not news.
- **Complication**: what changed, or what is wrong, in the audience's terms. This creates
  the question.
- **Question**: the one the complication raises; often implicit on the slide, but you
  should be able to write it.
- **Answer**: the governing thought.

Checks: the situation is uncontroversial (if the audience would dispute it, the deck has
to argue it first); the complication is theirs, not the author's; the question raised is
the one the answer answers; the answer comes on the first content page, not on page 12.
Minto's own name for the opening is the SCQ framework: its job is identifying the
question in the reader's mind. Her common patterns, each with its question: directives
("what should we do?"), requests for funds ("should we pay for this?"), how-to documents
("how do we do it?"), letters of proposal ("why you, and how?"), progress reviews ("where
are we?"; the answer is a judgement, not a list of activities), and choosing between
alternative solutions ("which one?").

The opening restates what the reader knows to set up the question; it does not bring
news. Minto: "there is no place for news in a document whose purpose is to communicate
your thinking". A situation slide that tells the audience something new and disputable
has to be argued like any other claim.

## Writing summary ideas: the "so what"

The commonest failure in a pyramid is a summary that does not summarise:

- **Intellectually blank assertions.** "There are three issues with the platform", "Five
  findings emerged", "We recommend seven actions". Minto: "Most writers simply group
  ideas, without completing the thinking", grouping by the plural noun ("five problems")
  rather than by a shared logic, and "topping off the lists with an intellectually blank
  assertion"; such assertions "are deadly for both the reader and the writer". Her course
  is blunter: "'There are three reasons' is not an appropriate insight!" Fix: state what
  the three have in common and what follows ("the platform's three outages share one
  cause: no owner for ingestion"). And when a title does say three, check there are three,
  not two or five.
- **Topic labels.** "Background", "Costs", "Timeline", "Q3 update". A label tells the reader
  what a slide is about, not what it says. Every title in the main flow is a sentence
  with a verb and a so-what.
- **Actions without results.** "The summary of the set of actions is always the effect
  of carrying out the actions": state the result the actions achieve, not the activity ("cut test runs to changed data only, saving a
  third of compute", not "optimise testing").
- **Situations without insight.** For a grouping of facts, the summary states what they
  have in common and what it implies, not "a number of trends".
- **The so-what test.** Read each title and ask "so what?". If the honest answer is a new
  sentence the audience needs, that sentence is the title.

## Group size

Minto's point is about memory (she cites the "magical number seven"): a reader cannot
hold a long list, so long lists must be grouped and the relationship stated. The working
convention in consulting is three to five key lines and three to five items per group;
it is a convention, not her rule, so treat a breach as a question. More than five usually
means two levels have been flattened into one (find the sub-groups) or that items of
different kinds are mixed. One item is not a group: either the level above is the same
idea or siblings are missing. Two items are fine when the structure is genuinely binary
(before and after, option A and B).

## On slides

The two logic tests are the pyramid read on a deck. Horizontal logic: read only the
titles, and together they tell the whole story, which needs action titles, not
descriptive ones. Vertical logic: everything on a slide supports its title. (C. N.
Knaflic, https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2013/12/horizontal-logic and
https://www.storytellingwithdata.com/blog/2013/12/vertical-logic.)
