# How BCG decks carry an argument

Pass 2 and Pass 3. What a reviewer holds a deck against: how information flows through a
BCG deck, how one slide holds one idea, how a sequence of slides builds a point, and which
exhibit proves which kind of message. Drawn from Nitin's local template libraries in
`${SLIDE_TEMPLATES_DIR}` (K, S, G, P as in consulting-slides' `pattern-library.md`; slide
numbers are positions in the file) and from BCG's published work (see `## Sources`).

## Checklist

Deck:
1. Map the deck onto cover, context, executive summary, sections (each opened so the
   reader knows where they are), key message slides, detail backup, decisions and next
   steps, appendix. Name what is missing, out of order, or doing two jobs.
2. Preliminary or estimated content says so where the number is. In his house look
   that is words on the slide ("estimate", "proposal", a `[placeholder]` with its date)
   and the green tab only for "Illustrative" and appendix labels (see below).

Each slide:
3. Say its one idea in a sentence without the title. Does the title say the same? Two
   sentences means two slides.
4. The title is a claim with a so-what, usually with the number that proves it; not a
   topic, a chart label or a count.
5. The exhibit proves that title and nothing else, and the title's figure is marked on
   it (ring, delta bubble, target line, dashed box). One accent element; the rest grey.
6. The exhibit's comparison (component, item, time, distribution, correlation, bridge,
   two-dimension) matches the title's comparison words.
7. Notes and sources: every figure traced; estimates marked where the number is; nothing
   in a note or bottom line undoes the title.
8. Key message slide or detail slide, and is it in the right place?

Across slides:
9. A point that spans pages builds one step at a time (overview, deep dives, summary in
   the same layout; progressive highlight; titles that chain; claim then evidence).
10. Markers, colours and icons mean the same thing on every page.
11. No list told twice in two versions; no jump the reader must bridge alone.

## 1. How information flows through a deck

The order is fixed, and every part has one job. Five public BCG decks (`## Sources`)
follow it closely:

1. **Cover**, then often a **context and objectives** page: what the document is for and
   what it is not (NYCHA p2; the IRA deck's objective page; Victoria's "note to the reader"
   setting out the data's limits before any finding).
2. **Executive summary**: the answer and its key lines. A reader who stops here has the
   whole argument. When the argument is too big for one page it runs to several, numbered
   "(I/IV)" to "(IV/IV)" (NYCHA p23 to 26), not crammed into one.
3. **Sections, one per key line.** Each opens with a tracker: the agenda repeated with
   the current section lit (Dallas repeats Context / Key findings / Recommendations / Next
   steps as a divider before each part; NYCHA repeats its contents page before each of
   five sections; K11, K19, K42, K62, K68, K82 and K86 in the K library are one contents
   page shown again and again with the next entry highlighted). The tracker can also be
   the deck's framework: Victoria brings back its "cascading value" chart at each section
   with the current layer in colour and every other layer greyed (p11, 28, 36).
4. **Key message slides** carry the main flow: one claim, one visual, one number lit.
5. **Detail slides** back them up, in the section or in the appendix, for the reader who
   asks "how do you know?".
6. **Decisions and next steps**: the ask, the owners, the dates.
7. **Appendix**: method, data, the full tables, the options ruled out. Opened by a divider,
   and organised so the reader can find the backup for any claim: Dallas gives each of its
   seven recommendations its own appendix page; NYCHA gives each function a current-state
   and a future-state page. Pages that are backup say so with a sticker ("Backup").

Stickers tell the reader how much weight a page bears: "Preliminary", "Draft", "For
discussion", "Illustrative", "Example", "Non-exhaustive list", "Backup" (NYCHA; Dallas
uses "Backup" 27 times). A recommendation or a figure that is still preliminary and says
so nowhere is a finding.

**His house rules win over the BCG habit here** (consulting-slides and `Style.md`): no
grey tracker text in the top corner; a section is opened by a divider or an agenda page
with the current part lit, not by a corner label; the green tab is used only where it
earns its place ("Illustrative" on a built example, "A1" on an appendix page). So a
missing corner tracker or a missing "Preliminary" sticker is not a finding on his decks.
What is a finding is a reader who cannot tell where they are in the argument, or cannot
tell an estimate from a fact. Fix those in his idiom: a divider, an agenda page, the
word "estimate", a dated placeholder.

Review: map the deck onto this order. Missing pieces (no summary, no close), pieces out of
order (methodology before the answer; detail before the claim it supports) and pieces doing
two jobs (a summary that is also the agenda) are findings.

## 2. Key message slides and detail slides

The K library is split in two: "Key Message slide examples" (K2 to K61) and "Detail slide
examples" (K67 to K92). Several messages appear in both forms, which shows the difference
better than any rule:

| Message | Key message form | Detail form |
|---|---|---|
| Savings found across countries | K6: the waterfall to the total, nothing else | K72: the same waterfall plus a row of savings rates per country and fuller labels |
| A client case | K63: two big multipliers and three short action lines | K95: situation, actions and impact as paragraphs with bullets |
| Three roles in coaching | K45: three icons, a name and one line each | K96: three columns of bullets with a summary box |
| Ways to grow | K48: three options under one heading, one line each | K98: the same three options with eligibility, timing and process |

What separates them:

- A key message slide states one claim, shows one exhibit that proves it at a glance,
  and lights one element. Usually under 50 words. The title can be read aloud as the point.
- A detail slide carries the evidence behind a claim already made: breakdowns, the
  full table, the method. It is dense on purpose and lives where the reader looks for
  proof, not where the presenter makes the point.
- The house rule says the same: main slides carry the elevated concept; specifics,
  counts and the dated record go to the appendix.

Review: for each slide decide which kind it is. A detail slide in the main flow of a
live meeting is a major finding (move it, and write the key message slide it supports).
A key message slide with no detail behind it, on a claim the audience will question, is
a finding too (the "how do you know?" has nowhere to go).

## 3. One idea per slide: the anatomy

| Part | Job | It fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Action title | States the slide's one claim, with its so-what, in a full sentence of at most two lines, usually with the number that proves it | It is a topic ("Market overview"), a chart label ("Employment by category, % of labour force"), a count ("We recommend seven actions"), two unrelated claims, or a question with no answer below |
| Lead-in line or panel headers | Scope, unit and period of the exhibit; on a two-panel slide, one headline per panel that together add up to the title | It restates the title, or argues a point the title does not make |
| Exhibit | Proves the title, and only the title | It shows something the title does not claim, or the claim needs a second exhibit |
| Highlight | Marks on the exhibit the exact figure the title states: a ring, a delta bubble, a target line, a dashed box round the rows that matter | Everything is coloured, the colour sits on the wrong element, or the title's number is nowhere on the chart |
| Takeaway or callout (optional) | The implication, when the title alone is not enough | It introduces a new idea |
| Notes and source | Numbered notes (method, sample, estimates, missing data), then "Source: ..., analysis" | Missing, "internal data", or a note that quietly contradicts the title |

A public example of every part working together, Dallas p13: the title "Reported dog
bites in Dallas up 15% annually from 2013-15 with bites from loose-owned dogs growing at
23%"; two panels with their own headlines (how a bite is recorded; bites are growing);
stacked bars with a "+15%" growth arrow and the "23%" ringed by hand in green, the two
numbers of the title; the estimated year labelled "est. 2016"; five numbered notes giving
the sample (n=4,290), the estimating method and the under-reporting caveat; then the
source. Note the title joins two figures with "with" and is still one message: the
second clause says where the first comes from. A conjunction is a question to ask, not a
verdict.

The Transform and Special Situations exhibit does the same in a publication: a sentence
title with two figures ("increased by 11pp ... 8pp higher than prepandemic averages"), a
capitalised chart header with unit and base ("index = 100"), grey delta bubbles carrying
exactly those two figures, a shaded band naming the period that explains them, and
"Sources: Eurostat; BCG analysis." with a note defining "pp".

BCG does not always pass its own test. Dallas p26 is titled "BCG recommends seven
actions for Dallas": a count, which Minto calls an intellectually blank assertion. The
insight sits in the subtitle instead ("recommendations must be taken as a whole; cherry
picking will not work"), and that subtitle was the title the slide needed. Hold a deck to
the best slides, not to the house average.

How the K library makes one idea visible, slide by slide:

- K4: historical bars in grey, the future bars in green, a single arrow with the growth
  rate needed. The claim is the gap between the two colours.
- K5: our line in green, the competitors' in grey, end labels instead of a legend. The
  claim is "we beat them"; nothing else is coloured.
- K10: a waterfall whose start and end bars are green and whose steps are grey. The claim
  is the total; the steps are the proof.
- K8: a staircase of grey steps with three ringed percentages over the three groups the
  claim is about.
- K9: stacked bars with one segment green in each, and the growth and margin of each
  segment in rings beside them. The claim is about that one segment.
- K20, K23: three or four big numbers in rings, each with a label; nothing else.

Not every slide in the libraries is a model. K7 and K8 carry a chart label where the
claim should be; K38, K41 and K81 use topic titles ("Client's growth journey until now",
"How we prioritize"). Hold a deck to the action-titled slides (K4, K5, K6, K10, K20,
K40), not to the library average.

Review for each slide:

1. Say the slide's one idea in a sentence without looking at the title. Does the title
   say the same thing? If you needed two sentences, it is two slides.
2. Cover the body. Does the title alone state a claim someone could disagree with?
3. Cover the title. Does the exhibit alone point to the same claim, by its highlight?
4. Is there anything on the slide that neither the title nor the proof needs? Cut it or
   move it.

## 4. Building a point across slides

A point too big for one slide is built across several, each adding exactly one thing.
The patterns, and how each fails:

- **Overview, deep dives, summary in the same layout.** The IRA deck (p14) shows six
  barriers as six columns with a dashed box "Example deep dives on following pages"; p15
  to 20 take one barrier each, every title opening with the barrier's name as a kicker
  ("Obstructive permitting | Permitting barriers can slow deployment by several years");
  p21 brings back the same six columns, now as levers. The reader always knows which part
  of the whole they are in. Fails when a deep dive drops the link back to the overview, or
  dives into a part the overview did not name.
- **Overview, then zoom.** The whole (a market, a system, a process) with one part
  highlighted, then that part in detail on the next slide, carrying the same highlight.
  Fails the same way.
- **Titles that chain.** Consecutive titles written as one sentence across two pages,
  joined by an ellipsis: "Taken together, the recommendations target a strategic shift to
  a new operating model..." then "...enabled by support functions operating at lower cost"
  (NYCHA p32 to 33); "Cultural tourism contributes $1b ... and has been growing..." then
  "...but still represents just 8% of the tourism industry" (Victoria p22, as two panel
  headers). The house rule asks the same of every title: connect to the previous claim in
  its terms.
- **Claim, then evidence.** Paired pages in a parallel layout: a claim page ("Arts-based
  approach has solid foundation") and its evidence page ("Key studies indicate strength of
  evidence"), repeated for each domain (Victoria). Fails when the pairs stop being parallel,
  so the reader cannot compare domain with domain.
- **Progressive highlight.** The same exhibit repeated, with the next element lit each
  time and the rest greyed; the agenda tracker and Victoria's framework are the
  deck-level cases. Fails when the exhibit changes shape between repeats, so the reader
  has to re-read it.
- **Build within a slide.** One worked example built click by click beside the full map
  (house rule: a flow gets one worked example built step by step). Fails when every step
  is shown at once, or when the build adds decoration instead of steps.
- **Same message, different form.** K65, K66 and K67 put one message ("two teams combine
  their strengths") as a Venn, as two columns, and as two columns joined by arrows. The
  Venn says "together", the columns say "side by side", the arrows say "matched". The
  form is part of the claim: check that the chosen form says what the title says.
- **Recurring marker.** The same icon, colour or numbered marker means the same idea on
  every slide (house rules: the same icon marks the same idea on consecutive slides;
  every body page points back to the summary's numbered levers). Fails when a marker is
  reused for a different idea, or an idea changes marker.
- **Before and after.** Two columns with the same row labels, today in grey and with the
  change in green. Fails when the rows differ, so the comparison is not like for like.
- **Claim, then proof, then implication.** A key message slide, its detail slide, then
  the slide that says what it means for the decision. Fails when the implication never
  comes and the section ends on evidence.

Review across slides: does each title connect to the previous slide's claim in its
terms (house rule)? Is any slide repeating the one before without adding anything? Is
there a jump (from problem to solution with no cause; from cost to plan with no options)
that the reader must bridge alone?

## 5. Which exhibit for which message

Start from the comparison the title makes (Gene Zelazny, *Say It With Charts*, McKinsey):

| The title says... | Comparison | Exhibit | Wrong choice that hides it |
|---|---|---|---|
| X is a share of the whole | component | one 100% bar, or a pie only when one slice is over half | a table of percentages |
| A ranks above B | item | horizontal bars, sorted | a pie; unsorted bars |
| It rose, fell or held | time series | columns for few periods, a line for many | a table of months |
| Most fall in this range | frequency | histogram | a line |
| More X goes with more Y | correlation | scatter, the point that matters labelled | two separate bar charts |

Zelazny's key words help: "share", "percentage of total" signal a component comparison;
"larger than", "ranks" an item comparison; "grew", "declined" a time series; "range",
"concentration" a distribution; "increases with", "varies with" a correlation. He
reckoned the pie the least practical of the five, a small share of the charts anyone
should use. If the title's comparison word and the chart's comparison differ, one of them
is wrong.

And the BCG staples, each for one kind of message, with a public example where one exists:

| Message | Exhibit | Seen in |
|---|---|---|
| How we get from A to B, step by step | waterfall (bridge): start and end lit, steps grey | NYCHA p42: total spend down to the in-scope base |
| A range against a target | paired waterfalls, low and high cases, with the target as a dashed line labelled with the title's figure | NYCHA p43 |
| Growth by component | stacked bars with a growth column beside them | Dallas p13 |
| Us against peers | grouped bars, the rows that matter in a dashed box | IRA p15 |
| Which to do first | effort-impact 2x2, marked "preliminary" until agreed | Dallas p28 |
| Cost against impact | bubble or scatter, the options labelled | Dallas p49 |
| Two shares at once (size of segment and mix within it) | Mekko (marimekko) | |
| Where things sit on two dimensions; which to back | 2x2 matrix (the growth-share matrix is BCG's own), the quadrant that matters shaded | |
| Options or domains scored on criteria | table with Harvey balls, dots or words; the recommended column marked | Victoria p38 |
| How the operating model changes | from/to table, or current-state and future-state pages | NYCHA p34 to 37 |
| A process or a phased plan | chevrons with the duration of each phase, the current step lit | NYCHA p6 |
| Who does what, when | Gantt with milestones and a today line | |
| Three or four headline results | big numbers in tiles | IRA p15 |
| What people said | one quote, verbatim, with role and date | |
| What we did and what it achieved | case slide: situation, actions, impact | K63, K95 |

Review: name the comparison the title makes and check the exhibit makes that comparison
obvious. A table where one row carries the message should be a chart of that row (the
table goes to the appendix). A chart with no highlighted element gives the eye nowhere to
land. A legend the reader has to decode, when direct labels would do, is a cost on every
read.

## 6. Colour, highlight and labels as argument

- One accent colour for the thing the title is about; grey for context. If two things
  are in the accent colour, the slide is making two points.
- Colour meaning is stable across the deck: green is always "ours" or "the proposal",
  grey always "today" or "the rest".
- Status colours always carry their word; a reader cannot argue with a colour.
- Direct labels on bars and line ends; a legend only when three or more series must be
  told apart.
- Annotations (a bracket with the growth rate, a ring on the key value, a "today" line)
  are the cheapest way to say what the reader should see. One or two per slide.

## 7. Sources and notes

BCG exhibits carry numbered notes, then a source line, under every chart: the data's
origin, its period, and "BCG analysis" when the figure is derived. The notes do real
work: the sample size, how an estimate was made, what was assumed for missing data, a
known bias (Dallas p13 says bites are likely under-reported). Estimates are labelled on
the chart itself ("est. 2016"; low and high cases), and a deck whose data has limits says
so before the first finding (Victoria's note to the reader). A slide pasted into another
deck still says where its figures came from.

Review: every figure on the page traces to a source line on that page; derived figures
say so; survey figures give the sample; estimates are marked where the number is, not
only in the notes; and no note or bottom line qualifies the title so heavily that the
title stops being true (the Columbia slide in `attack.md`).
