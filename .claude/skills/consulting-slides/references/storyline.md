# Storyline first: how consulting decks are argued

Read this before writing a single slide. A consulting deck is an argument, not a tour. The
titles carry the argument; the bodies prove the titles. Most of the quality is decided
before PowerPoint opens, so spend about 40% of the effort here (story), 30% on analysis,
30% on design.

## 1. Write the governing thought (answer first)

One sentence that states the recommendation or the conclusion the reader must leave with.
Bain calls it "answer first", McKinsey "the governing thought", Minto "the top of the
pyramid". Tests:

- It is a claim someone could disagree with, not a topic ("Revenue lands source by source
  through May '27 if every source runs in parallel", not "Data platform roadmap").
- It answers the question the audience actually has. Write that question down too.
- It can be said out loud in one breath.

If you cannot write it, you do not yet know what the deck is for. Go back to the analysis.

## 2. Open with SCQA

The executive summary (and often the first paragraph of a pre-read) follows
Situation, Complication, Question, Answer:

- Situation: a fact the audience already accepts ("We collect all the data").
- Complication: what changed or what is wrong, in their terms ("no number is true;
  analysts chase numbers 2.3 times as often as they analyse them").
- Question: the question that complication raises (often left implicit on the slide).
- Answer: the governing thought, then the 3 to 5 key lines that support it.

Tension builds S to C to Q and releases at A. A deck that opens with background, team
bios or methodology has not done this.

## 3. Build the pyramid

Under the governing thought sit 3 to 5 key lines. Each key line is itself a claim, and
under each sit the facts that prove it. Rules:

- Every level answers a question from the level above: "why?", "how?" or "how do you
  know?". Pick one question per level and keep it the same across siblings.
- Siblings are MECE: no overlap between them, nothing important missing. Test by asking
  what a sceptic would say is missing, and whether two lines could be merged.
- Order the siblings on purpose: deductively (premise, premise, conclusion), by time,
  by structure (business unit, region, source), or by importance (biggest first). Say
  which order you chose; the reader will feel it.
- Each line is a full sentence. Topic labels ("Costs", "Timeline") hide the argument.

## 4. Turn the pyramid into a dot-dash storyline

Before slides exist, write the storyline as an indented outline:

```
• Governing thought
  – Key line 1 (becomes a chapter or a slide title)
      · evidence, evidence, evidence (becomes the slide body)
  – Key line 2
      · ...
```

Dots are claims; dashes are what proves them. A dash with nothing under it is a slide you
cannot yet build; a dot with ten dashes is two slides.

## 5. Ghost deck, then the two logic tests

A ghost deck is the deck with titles only: every slide carries its action title and a
one-line note of the exhibit that will prove it. Review it with the audience's sponsor
before building anything. Two tests:

- Horizontal logic: paste every title into one document in order and read it top to
  bottom. It should read as the argument, with no gaps and no detours. Titles that read
  like a table of contents ("Overview", "Background", "Approach") fail.
- Vertical logic: on each slide, the body must prove the title and nothing else. If the
  exhibit shows something the title does not claim, change one of them.

## 6. Action title rules

- A complete sentence stating the "so what", in active voice, specific and quantified
  where possible: "Analysts chase numbers 2.3 times as often as they analyse them".
- At most two lines; about 15 words. Do not shrink the font to fit; cut words or split
  the slide.
- One message. A title with "and" usually hides two slides.
- The same grammar on every slide (all claims, or all questions with answers in the
  body). Do not alternate.
- No drama, no fake tension ("It's not X, it's Y"), no reframes. State the finding.
- The 60-second test: the presenter can explain the slide in a minute. The 5-second
  test: a reader skimming gets the point from the title and the one highlighted number.

## 7. Deck shapes that work

Pick one and keep it:

- Situation, impact, solution, improvement, next steps. Nitin's default for change
  proposals: what is true today, what it costs, what we propose, what gets better, what
  each person does. Appendix carries the detail.
- SCQA executive summary, then one chapter per key line, then decisions and next steps.
- Options, criteria, recommendation. For a decision between alternatives: the options,
  the criteria that matter, how each scores, the call.
- What, so what, now what. For status updates.

Size: a live 30-minute meeting carries about 12 to 15 body slides (1.5 to 2 minutes each)
plus appendix. A pre-read can be denser, because nobody is talking over it, but the
story rules do not change.

## 8. The two slides every deck needs

- Executive summary: the SCQA on one page, or the governing thought with the key lines
  numbered. The reader should be able to stop after this slide.
- Decisions and next steps: who decides what, and who does what by when. Named owners
  and dates, not "the team". Without this slide the meeting ends with nothing owned.

## 9. Review before sending

- Read titles only. Is the argument complete and in order?
- Each slide: does the exhibit prove the title? Is there one highlighted number?
- Executive summary and body agree on every number.
- Every quantitative claim has a source line; every acronym is expanded once.
- Nothing hedged ("we believe it may be possible"). Say it or cut it.
- Anything that does not carry the argument moved to the appendix.
