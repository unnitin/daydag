# Charts, tables and diagrams that prove a title

## Message first (Zelazny's method)

1. Write the message: the one sentence the reader must take from the chart. It becomes
   the slide title or the chart's lead-in.
2. Name the comparison the message makes. There are five:

| Comparison | The message says... | Chart |
|---|---|---|
| Component | share of a whole ("46 of 66 messages chase a number") | stacked bar, or a single 100% bar; a pie only if one slice is over half |
| Item | A ranks above B | horizontal bars, sorted |
| Time series | rose, fell, flat over periods | line for many periods, columns for few |
| Frequency distribution | how many fall in each range | column histogram |
| Correlation | more of X goes with more of Y | scatter / dot chart |

3. Pick the simplest chart that makes the comparison obvious. Bars, columns and lines cover
   most needs. Reach for a waterfall only for a bridge (start, steps, end), a Gantt only
   for a schedule, a 2x2 only for a positioning or trade-off, a Mekko only when two shares
   matter at once.

## Chart rules

- The chart proves the title. If the title says "twice as often", the two bars are the
  only thing coloured.
- One highlighted element in bright green; the rest grey. A chart where everything is
  the same colour gives the eye nowhere to land.
- Label values directly on bars and at line ends; drop the legend when there are three
  series or fewer. Drop gridlines or keep them a light grey hairline.
- Bars start at zero. Time runs left to right. Sort item comparisons by value unless the
  order has meaning.
- Units in the subtitle or axis title, once. Period in the subtitle ("Jan to Sep 2026").
- Round labels; no decimals the decision does not need.
- Text in the chart no smaller than 9 pt; axis labels 10 to 11 pt.
- Annotate: a bracket with "+11% p.a.", a circled number explained in a side note, a
  "today" marker. One or two annotations, not five.
- Source line under every chart.

## Tables

- At most 6 columns and about 8 rows on a slide; more goes to the appendix or splits.
- Header row shaded light grey, bold; hairline rules; no vertical lines.
- Numbers right-aligned with consistent decimals; text left-aligned; units in the header.
- First column is the thing being compared; totals in bold at the bottom.
- Use green check marks, Harvey balls or words for qualitative cells, never colour alone.
- A table is for lookup. If the slide has one message, chart the one row that carries it
  and move the table to the appendix.

## Diagrams

- Process: chevrons or numbered circles on a rail, 3 to 6 steps, one line of text each,
  the current step in green.
- Timeline / roadmap: periods as columns (months or quarters), one lane per workstream,
  bars for work and diamonds or pills for milestones, a shaded "today" column, a legend
  that uses words as well as colour (built, in progress, planned).
- Framework: the house (purpose on the roof, pillars, foundation), the pyramid, the
  funnel, concentric rings, the 2x2. Label every part; no empty boxes.
- Flow: boxes left to right with thin arrows; at most 7 boxes across; one lane per actor
  if hand-offs matter.
- Hub: one key in the centre with the things it joins around it. Good for "everything on
  one key" stories.
- Before / after: two columns with the same row labels, "today" in grey and "with the
  change" in green.

## KPI tiles and big numbers

- Three or four across; 40 to 60 pt figure, 11 to 12 pt label beneath; the figure in dark
  green or in a ring.
- Each number has a unit and a period; estimates are marked ("~", "to confirm").
- Do not tile numbers that are not comparable or not decision-relevant ("data slop").

## Quotes

- One quote per slide, 18 to 24 pt, in quotation marks, with the speaker, role and date
  beneath. Verbatim; never tidied. Pair with a one-line "what it means" if the quote is
  being used as evidence for a decision.

## When the data is thin

Say so on the slide. A strawman chart labelled as a strawman is honest; an unlabelled one
is a fabrication. Never invent a number to fill a tile.
